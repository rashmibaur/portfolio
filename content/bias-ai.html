Fundamentals

Understanding Artificial Intelligence

A structured overview of what artificial intelligence is – and what it is not. Task-specific systems vs. theoretical general intelligence.

What is AI?

Task-specific intelligence.

  • Generative AI refers to artificial intelligence systems that can create new content, including text, images, audio, video, and code.
  • GenAI learns patterns from vast amounts of training data and generates outputs based on user prompts.
  • Designed to perform specific functions or solve particular problems.
  • Excels in defined domains but cannot transfer knowledge across different tasks.
vs
What is not AI?

Human-like, general-purpose intelligence (still theoretical).

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

  • Ability to understand, learn, and apply knowledge across any domain.
  • Can reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, and learn from experience like humans.
  • Can transfer learning from one context to another seamlessly.
Current state
Theoretical concept, not yet achieved.
Timeline
Experts debate whether AGI is decades or centuries away.

Taxonomy

Types of biases

AI systems can exhibit various forms of bias, reflecting inequalities present in society and training data.

  • Racial / ethnic bias

    Misidentification and stereotyping in facial recognition, hiring, and loan applications.

    01

  • Socioeconomic bias

    Reinforces inequalities by favoring higher-income individuals in loan approvals, education, and targeted advertising.

    02

  • Geographic / location bias

    Poor performance for underrepresented regions, affecting navigation apps and disaster response systems.

    03

  • Age bias

    Discrimination against older or younger individuals in credit scoring, job applications, and healthcare recommendations.

    04

  • Disability bias

    Exclusion through inaccessible interfaces, biased hiring algorithms, and medical tools that don't account for diverse health conditions.

    05

  • Cultural bias

    Struggles with non-dominant cultures, leading to misinterpretations and offensive outputs in language models and image generators.

AI fairness · strategies for equitable systems

Bias mitigation strategies

Bias can be addressed at three points in a model's life: in the data, while it learns, and in what it outputs. This project focuses on pre-processing.

1

Before training

Pre-processing

Address bias in the data before model training begins.

  • Diverse data collectionensure representation across all demographic groups.
  • Bias auditingidentify underrepresented groups and missing perspectives.
  • Data augmentationgenerate synthetic data to balance representation.
  • Feature selectionremove features that act as proxies for sensitive attributes.
2

During training

In-processing

Integrate fairness directly into the model training process.

  • Fairness constraintsincorporate fairness metrics into optimization objectives.
  • Adversarial debiasinguse adversarial networks to remove discriminatory patterns.
  • Multi-task learningoptimize for both accuracy and fairness simultaneously.
  • Reweighting samplesbalance group representation during training.
3

After training

Post-processing

Adjust model outputs to achieve fairness after development.

  • Threshold optimizationadjust decision thresholds for different groups.
  • Calibrationensure prediction reliability across all groups.
  • Output modificationadjust outputs to satisfy fairness constraints.

Measurement framework

How to measure these biases

Ten measures, each with the question it answers and a worked example.

  1. 01

    Demographic parity

    Equal outcome rates across groups.

    Example60% of both men and women are hired.

  2. 02

    Equalized odds

    Equal accuracy (TPR & FPR) across groups.

    ExampleLoan approval correctly identifies 80% of qualified applicants in both male and female groups.

  3. 03

    Equal opportunity

    Equal true positive rates for qualified individuals.

    Example90% of qualified students are admitted, regardless of gender.

  4. 04

    Individual fairness

    Similar individuals receive similar predictions.

    ExampleTwo people with identical qualifications receive the same loan decision, regardless of race.

  5. 05

    Word weights

    Shows which words influence model outputs.

    ExampleFor the prompt “a nurse,” attention is highest on “woman,” revealing gender bias.

  6. 06

    Calibration

    Predicted probabilities match actual outcomes.

    Example70% predicted disease risk = 70% actual occurrence for all demographics.

  7. 07

    Counterfactual fairness

    Predictions unchanged if sensitive attributes are altered.

    ExampleA female candidate's hiring decision remains the same if gender is changed to male.

  8. 08

    Simpson's diversity index

    Probability of selecting different categories; higher = more diverse.

    ExampleBalanced gender output (D=0.5) vs. male-dominated output (D=0.095).

  9. 09

    Word embeddings

    Reveals stereotypical word associations in AI models.

    Example“Doctor” is closer to “man” than “woman” in vector space.

  10. 10

    Shannon entropy

    Measures diversity in outputs; higher = more diverse.

    Example50 male/50 female images (H=1.0) vs. 95 male/5 female (H=0.29).

AI fairness

Biases in the real world reflect biases in models

AI models learn from real-world data. When that data reflects societal biases, the AI inherits and amplifies them, leading to skewed and unfair outputs.

001

Demographic & identity biases

  • Racial/ethnicfavoritism in visual and language outputs.
  • Genderprofessions linked to specific genders; misclassification of non-binary individuals.
  • Agestereotypical depictions and underrepresentation of age groups.
  • Disabilitylimited representation and stereotypical portrayals.
002

Social & cultural biases

  • Socioeconomicbias based on economic status, education, and social class.
  • GeographicWestern-centric values; underrepresentation of other regions.
  • Culturalhomogenized visual culture; limited diversity in practices.
  • Appearanceattractiveness associated with specific physical traits.
003

Gender activity binding (GAB) bias

AI links activities to gender stereotypes from training data, causing:

  • A 13.2% accuracy drop for counter-stereotypical scenarios.
  • Difficulty recognizing multiple genders in the same activity.
  • Perpetuation of harmful stereotypes in hiring and media.

The result – biased data creates biased AI, reinforcing existing inequalities.
