Background

What are electoral bonds?

Electoral bonds were a mode of funding for political parties in India from their introduction in 2017.

Electoral bonds, resembling promissory notes, are interest-free banking instruments purchasable by any Indian citizen or registered organization, subject to RBI's KYC norms. Available in various denominations (one thousand, ten thousand, one lakh, ten lakh, one crore), they can be obtained from specific State Bank of India branches through cheque or digital payments. Donors must redeem these bonds within 15 days in the designated account of a registered political party that secured at least 1% of the votes in the last election.

Objectives

Objectives of electoral bonds

The government defended introducing electoral bonds in the Supreme Court, aiming to enhance accountability, combat “black money,” and promote a cashless economy. They highlighted the bonds' limited timeframe and donor record-keeping for transparency. Electoral bonds incentivize donations via the banking channel, capturing donor information for accountability and electoral reform.

Impact

Impact on existing regulations

  • Contribution limit for registered political parties reduced to ₹2,000 from ₹20,000, a reduction to 10%, via Finance Act, 2017.
  • Electoral bonds remove the ceiling on corporate contributions, previously limited to 7.5% of average net earnings over three years, as per Companies Act, 2013 amendment.
  • Mandatory disclosure of detailed political contributions eliminated; companies now only need to disclose a consolidated sum for electoral bonds purchase, per amendments to Income Tax Act, 1961.
  • FCRA amended to broaden the definition of “foreign” entities, expanding the scope of firms to include branches or subsidiaries of companies abroad that were allowed to make political contributions with support from both government and opposition.

Criticism

Criticism of electoral bonds

  • Electoral bonds allow anonymous corporate donations to political parties without limitations.
  • Critics argue this lack of transparency grants undue influence to corporations and obscures connections between politicians and businesses.
  • Limited public documentation on purchasers and recipients, coupled with SBI's records, raises concerns about government influence, granting an unfair advantage.
  • Concerns exist about shell companies facilitating contributions and the potential exploitation of donor information by the ruling government.
  • There's a risk of undermining democratic elections through untraceable funds transferred to voters' accounts.
  • The money can be used for any purpose after withdrawal, as there is no requirement for parties to disclose how it's spent.

Termination

Termination of the electoral bonds scheme

On February 15, 2024, a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court of India, unanimously struck down the electoral bonds scheme and associated amendments to the Representation of People Act, Companies Act, and Income Tax Act as unconstitutional. They deemed it a violation of the Right to Information (RTI) and voters' right to information about political funding under Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution, highlighting concerns about potential quid pro quo arrangements between corporations and politicians.

Legal challenges

Legal challenges leading up to termination

The electoral bonds scheme was subjected to legal challenge through a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in the Supreme Court of India on two grounds.

  1. Lacks transparencyThe scheme lacks transparency in political funding, hindering access to vital information for the Election Commission and citizens.
  2. Money BillPassing the scheme as a Money Bill bypasses the Rajya Sabha, violating the doctrine of separation of powers and citizens' fundamental right to information, essential aspects of the Constitution's basic structure.

The Supreme Court reviewed petitions filed by the Association for Democratic Reforms (represented by Prashant Bhushan) and the Communist Party of India (Marxist). They challenged the legality of modifications to several acts through the Finance Acts of 2016 and 2017, including the Reserve Bank of India Act, the Representation of the People Act, the Income Tax Act, the Companies Act, and the Foreign Contribution Regulation Act. These amendments facilitated the introduction of electoral bonds.

The data

Data released by the Election Commission of India

  • On March 11, 2024, the Supreme Court instructed the State Bank of India to disclose electoral bond details to the Election Commission of India by the following day's close of business. This data, encompassing all bonds encashed from April 12, 2019, to January 24, 2024, was subsequently published on the ECI's website on March 15, 2024.
  • The Supreme Court reprimanded the State Bank of India (SBI), directing it to disclose all electoral bond details, including unique bond numbers revealing the buyer-party link, by March 21.

Interactive data narrative

What the data says

Nine findings from the released data. Each chart is interactive – hover and click to explore.

01

Most electoral bonds were donated to and encashed by ruling parties, whether at the central or state level, indicating their significant influence in electoral funding.

02

Half of all electoral bonds came from just 20 companies, potentially influencing policy decisions and political outcomes.

03

By utilizing the unique ID numbers of bonds, a linkage was established between donor companies and political parties, revealing the correlation between companies contributing larger sums and major political parties.

04

Nearly 90% of purchased bonds were in denominations of 1 crore, suggesting that larger corporations acquired multiple bonds of this value.

05

Although bonds were acquired from diverse SBI branches, a significant majority were encashed in the central capital, indicating a connection between the received bonds and the ruling party.

06

The majority of companies purchased a limited number of bonds and donated them to one or two political parties. However, companies acquiring larger bond sums tended to donate to a wider array of parties.

07

The bulk of listed companies directed their electoral bond donations towards larger political parties. This trend underscores the inclination of corporate entities to align themselves with major political players, potentially seeking to secure favorable policies or relationships with parties holding significant sway in the political arena.

08

The predominant trend observed in the encashment of bonds by political parties occurred either during or in the month preceding major state elections. By timing their bond encashments strategically around significant state elections, parties may seek to capitalize on heightened political engagement and public attention to maximize their electoral prospects.

09

Certain companies have purchased electoral bonds exceeding their net profits, indicating the potential existence of shell companies or the utilization of illegal sources of unregistered income.

Perspective

What else could have been done with the total amount of electoral bonds?

A proposal

What if we established a Common National Election Fund, where both companies and individuals could contribute?

The collected funds would then be distributed equally among various candidates and political parties, taking into account their strength in the Parliament or Legislature, as well as other factors like their electoral performance. This system would ensure a fair and level playing field for all candidates and parties involved.

Moreover, those who donate to the Common National Election Fund would receive tax incentives. To prevent undue influence, there would be limitations on the amount companies can contribute, based on their performance in recent financial years. This measure would effectively eliminate donations from shell companies, reduce corruption, and mitigate quid pro quo situations.

Overall, implementing such a system would not only uphold the principles of democracy but also encourage transparency and fairness in our electoral process.
